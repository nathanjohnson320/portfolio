State of the Programming Union

October 5, 2026

To preface this, I do not use AI for any of these posts. They are poorly written with bad grammar by yours truly.

With that, hello, it's been a while.

Right now the current state of the programming union is as follows

  • layoffs++++
  • hiring fill time+++++++
  • burnout+++++++++++++
  • fulfillment-------

Let me elaborate on some of these.

Layoffs & Hiring Time

I see a lot of doom and gloom with software these days in the era of AI. Some of this is warranted, some isn't. At a high level I can see lots of my former colleagues (and current colleagues) being terminated from their positions due to reorgs and redundancy changes. My current position is no different I am certain at some point I will be eliminated. If you are unfortunately laid off, you may also see that getting hired takes exponentially longer than it used to. My friends used to lock in a position in a few weeks after applying to 5 or 6 places. Now you submit 400 applications and maybe hear back from 20 and get 1 or 2 interviews. AI is flooding jobs with bogus applications because it's easy to lie and nobody is solving that half of the problem.

Burnout & Fulfilloment

Even with that I still have some level of excitement about engineering in general. As AI improves, the cost to generate ideas drops further and further. Will the cost stand the test of time (ehhhhhhhhhh 20b loss from anthropic last year?) I don't think it will. Eventually we may see some form of government subsidization if things get bad enough but I doubt that likely we'll see a correction in the cost of hardware or local LLMs may at some point improve to the point where we don't need the cloud providers anymore. I'm not sure. But anyways regarless of that I am absolutely feeling the burnout. It takes me 30 seconds to prompt cursor to pull a ticket and implement it while I much on a bag of chips. So if I am purely a prompt monkey knocking out a checklist without interacting with any humans then it's logically only a matter of time until I am eliminated. That's probably wrong though, I feel like niche expertise allows you some level of job security especially the way AI fails to understand brownfield projects. Maybe 1-2 years down the road at the curreent rate this won't be the case but we will see.

Where are we going

This is my take, and I'll probably be completely wrong, but I see AI continuing the exponential rise, engineers phasing into a different role where we're more product level focus instead of low level code focus, and what we used to know as a software engineer will go away. Maybe it will be called product engineering or something like this who knows. But at the very least the actual engineer salary will go down, the number of engineers will go down, and then and only then when AI has nothing left to train on but the remaining AI slop people will come back to software engineering. But again who knows we could get hit by an asteroid anyday.

How do we feel again

So one last thing, if you're like me and burnt out. The best way I've found to get around that is by turning off the AI and hand crunching a problem. Not all problems. But specifically the problems that you personally find interesting. A quick example of this, I recently implemented atomvm_gleam which is just wrappers for atomvm's erlang APIs. I don't want to write that and I find it incredibly boring. What I find _not_ boring is to implement things on top of atomvm_gleam even very basic things like a blinky light on an ESP32 microcontroller. Could I do that in 3 seconds with an LLM? Sure, but that doesn't make me happy so I'm not going to do it. Also go to conferences because I just attended goatmire and it restored my faith in the elixir community.
